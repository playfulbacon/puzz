# What makes Japanese logic puzzles work, and what makes them good

Research notes to start a project on themed, aesthetic, Nikoli-style puzzles.

How sure each part is: statements with a link come from the sources listed at the end. Parts marked *(synthesis)* are my own reading of the craft, pulled from those sources and from common practice among puzzle setters. I couldn't open Bellos's Guardian columns or the text of *Puzzle Ninja* directly, so what's here about his work comes from the book's own description and from secondary sources.

---

## 1. What a "Japanese logic puzzle" is

The form was mostly shaped by **Nikoli**, the magazine and publisher Maki Kaji founded in 1980. It popularised Sudoku and invented or refined dozens of genres, including Slitherlink, Nurikabe, Heyawake, Masyu, Akari, Hashiwokakero, Shikaku and LITS. The defining traits:

| Trait | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Simple rules** | Kaji said the secret to inventing a good puzzle was to make the rules simple. Naoki Inaba has invented more than 400 genres, and his rules "can often be summed up in a single line." |
| **Exactly one solution** | Every puzzle has one answer, and you reach it by deduction from the clues. |
| **No guessing** | A well-made puzzle can be finished without trial and error. Difficulty comes from how deep the reasoning goes, not from guessing and backtracking. |
| **No outside knowledge** | "Culture-independent": no vocabulary and no trivia. Numbers and symbols are just markers, and any arithmetic is minimal. For Area Mazes, Inaba let solvers use width × height and nothing else, "especially [not] fractions and equations." |
| **Hand-made** | Nikoli puzzles are set by people, not generated by computer. Its editors review every puzzle, and experienced setters still get rejected. Nikoli describes solving as "a sort of mind game between the solver and the puzzle creator." |
| **Grid, pencil, paper** | A small board, a few mark types (fill, dot, line, number) and a clean finished state. |

Bellos's framing in *Puzzle Ninja* (2017): in Japan, logic puzzles are treated as an art form. A community of setters writes them by hand "with a view to aesthetic as well as mathematical beauty." He calls them "probably the most pleasurable puzzles ever invented." The book collects over 200 puzzles from setters he met in Tokyo, among them Kaji and the "puzzle poet" whose name became a Sudoku technique (Tetsuya Nishio). It also introduces 20+ genres such as Shakashaka and Marupeke.

## 2. Two levels of design: the genre and the puzzle

Nikoli's culture separates two creative acts. Keeping them apart matters for this project:

1. **Genre invention**: writing the rules. Since 1988 Nikoli has run the **"omopa"** section, where readers submit new genres and the editors publish the few that work. More than 450 have appeared there. Only a handful became classics.
2. **Setting**: making one particular grid in an existing genre. This is where flow, the entry point and elegance live.

A themed puzzle can work at either level. A new genre can have a theme built into its rules, or an individual grid can carry a theme in an existing genre.

## 3. What makes a genre good

- **You can learn the rules at a glance.** One or two sentences, with no exceptions and no special cases.
- **Techniques emerge from the rules.** Working out how to solve a new genre is "its own puzzle." Good rules produce recurring patterns that the rules never state, such as adjacent 3s in Slitherlink or the pools and walls of Nurikabe. The solver discovers these patterns, which gives a genre depth over time.
- *(synthesis)* **Local clues combined with a global rule.** Most classics pair a local count ("this island has 4 cells") with a global condition: one connected sea, a single loop, every cell lit, no 2×2 pools. The global rule makes distant parts of the grid affect each other, which is where the interesting deductions come from. Nurikabe's three rules fit this pattern exactly.
- **Clues are cheap to place.** Setters need room to control difficulty. If every clue gives away a lot, puzzles are trivially easy. If every clue gives away very little, puzzles need crowded grids.
- **Fun to set as well as to solve.** Nikoli is run by its fans, and solvers become setters. Genres that survive give setters room to show a personal style.

## 4. What makes an individual puzzle good

Taken from Nikoli's stated values, the Grandmaster Puzzles "Best of" criteria, and writing on hand-made versus generated puzzles:

1. **A clear way in.** There's an obvious first move, often visually signposted. Easy puzzles are easy "because the puzzler leads you through."
2. **Flow.** Each deduction opens the next one. GM Puzzles praises grids that "follow a nicely hidden logical path throughout" and where "after initial work, things flow smoothly along." The setter "knows just when to encourage and when to withhold."
3. **The setter's voice.** In a hand-made grid, the solver picks up on the author steering them toward what to look for next. A generated grid has no such voice, so solving becomes a mechanical routine. This is the main argument for hand-setting.
4. **An aha moment, or logical theme.** The best puzzles centre on one striking deduction, a "fresh" or "emergent" piece of logic that the whole grid is built to show off.
5. **Few clues.** Every clue earns its place, and GM Puzzles praises "very minimal clues."
6. **Visual elegance.** Nikoli made **rotationally symmetric givens** standard in Sudoku (reportedly also requiring ≤32 givens). Symmetry doesn't change the logic, but it tells the solver the grid was made on purpose. One reviewer said the layout "invites me to come in and solve it." Computer-generated grids are "the family station wagon"; hand-made ones are "a fancy European sports car."
7. **Difficulty through depth, not tedium.** Hard puzzles need sharper insight, not longer bookkeeping and not guessing.
8. *(synthesis)* **A good ending.** Tension should build toward the key deduction and then resolve. The last part of the solve shouldn't drag.

## 5. "Theme" already means three things. We want the third.

1. **Logical theme**: the puzzle is built around a particular deduction. ("A Liar Slitherlink with a Bull's-Eye theme… highlighting several different uses of the lying clues.")
2. **Visual theme, or "clue art"**: the givens form a picture, letter or pattern. Thomas Snyder's *The Art of Sudoku* is the reference: "artistic and logical themes that could never have been produced by random computer generation."
3. **Fictional or aesthetic theme**: a world, a set of objects and a mood. This is what this project is after, and the classic literature says least about it.

### It's already there in the classics, quietly

Many Nikoli genres are small metaphors, and the metaphor carries the rules:

- **Nurikabe** (ぬりかべ): a yokai, an invisible wall that blocks travellers. You build the wall (the sea) around the islands.
- **Hashiwokakero**, "build bridges!": islands joined by bridges that can't cross each other. The fiction explains the rule.
- **Akari**, "light": lamps light along rows and columns, and two lamps can't shine on each other.
- **Heyawake**, "divided rooms": shaded cells can't split the rooms off from each other.
- **Tentai Show**, "astronomical show": regions that are symmetric around a star. The finished grid looks like galaxies.
- **Hitori**, "alone" / "leave me alone".

Outside Nikoli: **Tents** (trees and tents), **Star Battle**, and **Nonograms / Oekaki-logic**, where the payoff is a picture. The video game **Islands of Insight** puts Nikoli genres such as Nurikabe into a painterly world of floating islands.

## 6. Principles for themed, aesthetic puzzles *(synthesis)*

1. **The theme has to do real work.** The best themes explain the rules: bridges can't cross, light travels in straight lines, water runs downhill, a garden path can't branch. If you could swap the fiction for numbers and nothing would change, the theme is just decoration. If the fiction predicts the rules, the solver learns faster and remembers longer.
2. **Keep it culture-independent.** The theme helps intuition but never carries information. A solver who knows nothing about koi, tea ceremonies or constellations must still be able to solve the puzzle from the one-line rules.
3. **One sentence of rules, stated in the fiction.** "Every lantern lights its row and column until a wall; no two lanterns see each other." If you need a paragraph, the genre isn't ready.
4. **Make the finished grid beautiful.** Aim for a solved state that *looks like the thing*: a raked gravel garden, a river system, a constellation, a woven textile. This is where themed puzzles can beat abstract ones. But a picture constraint fights uniqueness and flow (a common weakness of nonograms), so the logic comes first and the picture has to come out of it.
5. **Readable glyphs.** Themed symbols can replace numbers and marks, but a count must still read as a count at a glance, and every cell state must be easy to tell apart. Clear information comes before decoration.
6. **Build the aesthetic into the grid itself.** Symmetry, few clues and negative space look good in any genre. They also fit Japanese design sensibilities such as *ma*, the meaningful empty space.
7. **Match the aha to the story.** A grid's logical theme can be its narrative moment: the one bridge that must exist, the lamp that lights the whole garden.
8. **Hand-set, machine-checked.** Nikoli sets by hand, but a solver or uniqueness checker is standard practice among modern setters. It keeps the human voice and catches broken puzzles.

## 7. Checklist for each puzzle

- [ ] Rules fit in one or two sentences, stated in the theme's own terms
- [ ] Exactly one solution (machine-verified)
- [ ] Solvable without guessing; the hardest step is identified and deliberate
- [ ] Clear way in, visible from the starting grid
- [ ] Solve flows; no long flat stretches of bookkeeping
- [ ] At least one aha deduction, ideally tied to the theme
- [ ] Every clue is needed (remove any clue and the puzzle breaks or goes non-unique)
- [ ] Givens are visually composed (symmetric or deliberately arranged)
- [ ] Solving needs no outside knowledge
- [ ] Solved grid is satisfying to look at
